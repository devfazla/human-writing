# Platform notes: what the machine version looks like, and what to do instead

Contents: General rules · Instagram · Facebook · LinkedIn · Hashnode and other dev blogs

These come from the research in `detectors.md`, from widely published guides to spotting AI-written social posts (several of which agree on the same patterns), and from the case in `case-studies.md`. They are tendencies, not laws. Platform norms also move, so trust the user's own past posts over this file when they conflict.

## General rules

- Build every variant from the user's facts. Don't write one master post and trim or stretch it.
- Give each platform a different shape, not just a different length. Same skeleton across four posts is a pattern.
- Specifics carry the post: the stack, the number, the step, the name. A reader should learn something a competitor's post couldn't say.
- Hashtags are fine in the user's usual style. In the case study, an identical hashtag line sat in both the flagged and the passing version, so it wasn't the problem. Keep to 3 to 5 relevant ones.
- A call to action only when the user asked for one, and then as a plain instruction ("join the waitlist at ..."), not a question aimed at engagement.

## Instagram

Machine version: a hook line, emoji at the start of lines, a feelings sentence, a "stay tuned" ending, a wall of 15+ hashtags.
Human version: short, plain, concrete. What exists, what's next, the link, a few hashtags. Under about 80 words is normal. Write less, not more (see the short-post rule in SKILL.md).

## Facebook

Machine version: "Excited to announce", exclamation marks, a bulleted feature list with emoji, "Stay tuned for more!".
Human version: a few ordinary sentences, the way you'd tell people who know you. Say what happened, what's working, what's next. The audience is often friends and clients, so plain talk beats pitch language.

## LinkedIn

Machine version (the most heavily catalogued): a one-line hook built to stop the scroll, every sentence on its own line, "Here's what I learned" or "3 lessons", a tasteful vulnerability story with no concrete detail, bold one-liners, an engagement question at the end ("What's your take?"), and rule-of-three or "Not X. Y." constructions repeated through the post.
Human version: open with the thing itself. A longer first paragraph, a shorter middle, and a plain last line that doesn't ask for anything. Merge one-line paragraphs into real ones. Put in one number, one date, one name that's actually the user's. Take a position only if the user holds one. Skip the closing question.

## Hashnode and other developer blogs

Machine version: "In this article, we'll explore...", a table of contents for a 600-word post, Title Case headings for every section, a "Key Takeaways" list, a "Conclusion" that restates the intro, and an invitation to comment.
Human version: start with what was built and why, then how, with the real details a developer wants: versions, libraries, hosting, what was awkward, what's unfinished. Code or config snippets only if the user gave them. Use few headings, sentence case, and none of them named "Introduction" or "Conclusion". End when the last concrete point ends. A dev reader trusts a post that names the exact stack and admits the interim hack (for example, a temporary email setup) more than one that says "robust and scalable".
